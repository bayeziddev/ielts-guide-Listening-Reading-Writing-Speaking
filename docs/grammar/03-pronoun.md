# 3. Pronoun

> **Chapter focus:** reference, agreement, relative clauses, and formal clarity.

## How to study this lesson

Read the explanation first, then say the examples aloud. Change one detail in each example, such as the subject, time, or place. Complete the practice without looking back and use the answer key to understand the reason for each answer. Finish by writing two sentences about your own life or city.

A **pronoun** stands for a noun or noun phrase. It prevents unnecessary repetition, but the reader must always be able to identify its reference.

### Main pronoun groups

**Personal pronouns** include *I, you, he, she, it, we, they* and their object forms *me, him, her, us, them*. Use the subject form before a verb: **She and I discussed the issue**. Use the object form after a verb or preposition: **The teacher advised us** and **between you and me**.

**Possessive forms** are *my, your, his, her, its, our, their* before a noun and *mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs* when the noun is omitted: **This is their proposal; that one is ours**. **Its** has no apostrophe.

**Reflexive pronouns** (*myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, themselves*) refer back to the subject: **The participants introduced themselves**. Do not use *myself* simply because it sounds formal: **Please contact me**, not **Please contact myself**.

**Relative pronouns** introduce information about a noun: *who* for people, *which* for things, and *that* for people or things in defining clauses. **The researcher who conducted the survey published the results.** Use a comma before a non-defining clause: **The survey, which was conducted online, attracted 500 responses.**

**Indefinite pronouns** include *someone, anyone, nobody, each, every, both, either,* and *neither*. *Each, every, someone,* and *nobody* are grammatically singular: **Every participant has a choice**.

### Clear reference

Avoid a pronoun when its reference is unclear: **When the device was placed beside the phone, it overheated** does not clearly say what overheated. Repeat the noun or rewrite the sentence: **The device overheated when it was placed beside the phone**.

### Practice

Choose the best form: (a) The manager asked my colleague and (I/me) to present. (b) Every applicant must bring (their/his or her) passport. (c) The city introduced a scheme, (who/which) reduced congestion.

**Answers:** (a) **me**, (b) **their** is widely used as a singular gender-neutral pronoun in modern English; **his or her** is also grammatically possible, (c) **which**.

## Deep lesson: make every pronoun point clearly

A pronoun replaces a noun, but a good pronoun must have an obvious **antecedent**—the noun it refers to. If the reader has to guess what *it*, *this*, or *they* means, your cohesion is weak.

### Form chart

| Subject | Object | Possessive before noun | Possessive alone |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| I | me | my | mine |
| he | him | his | his |
| she | her | her | hers |
| we | us | our | ours |
| they | them | their | theirs |

Use a subject before a verb: **She and I agree.** Use an object after a verb or preposition: **The teacher helped us** and **between you and me**.

### Twelve examples

1. **The city** expanded, and **it** needed better roads.
2. **The students** practised, so **they** improved.
3. The tutor gave **me** useful advice.
4. **Their** proposal was stronger than **ours**.
5. The participants introduced **themselves**.
6. Everyone should bring **their** passport; singular *they* is natural and inclusive.
7. **Each** of the options has a cost; *each* is singular.
8. The device overheated after **it** was covered.
9. The survey **which** was conducted online attracted 800 people.
10. The researcher **who** led the project published the results.
11. The policy, **which** was controversial, was approved.
12. The report explains **what** the committee recommends.

### Relative pronouns

Use **who** for people and **which** for things. Use **that** in a defining clause: **The method that reduced waste was adopted.** A defining clause identifies the noun and has no commas. A non-defining clause adds extra information and uses commas: **The method, which reduced waste, was adopted.** Do not use *that* after a comma in formal standard English.

### Common errors

**Wrong:** Me and my friend completed the task. **Right:** **My friend and I completed the task.**
**Wrong:** The policy helped farmers, this increased income. **Right:** **The policy helped farmers, and this increased income** or **This policy increased farmers’ income.**
**Wrong:** The city introduced a scheme who reduced traffic. **Right:** a scheme **that/which** reduced traffic.

### Practice

A. Choose: **The manager asked my colleague and (I/me) to present.** Answer: **me**, because it is the object of *asked*.

B. Repair: **When the phone was placed beside the laptop, it overheated.** Answer: **The laptop overheated when the phone was placed beside it** if the laptop is meant.

C. IELTS task: Write a paragraph about a public policy. Use *it*, *they*, one relative clause, and one possessive form. Circle every pronoun and draw an arrow to its antecedent.

## Professor's explanation: how to think about this grammar

Do not begin by memorising a definition. Begin by asking three questions: **What job does this form do? What meaning does it add? Where does it go in the sentence?** Bengali and English often arrange information differently, so direct translation can create mistakes. Build the English pattern first, then attach your idea to it.

## Bengali learner warning

Many Bengali speakers understand the meaning of an English sentence but omit a small grammar word because Bengali does not always need the same word. English normally needs a clear subject and a finite verb. For example, say **The number of students is increasing**, not **Number of students increasing**. Treat articles, auxiliary verbs, plural endings, and prepositions as part of the pattern, not as optional decoration.

## Three-step study method

**Step 1 — Notice:** underline the target form in every example. **Step 2 — Control:** replace only one part of the example, such as the subject or time. **Step 3 — Communicate:** use the form in a short answer about education, health, transport, technology, or the environment. If you cannot explain why you chose the form, return to Step 1.

## Practice ladder

**Level 1: Recognition.** Find the target form and name its job. **Level 2: Controlled production.** Complete or correct a sentence using the pattern. **Level 3: IELTS production.** Write a short paragraph or speak for one minute. At Level 3, clarity is more important than using many different forms.
