# 5. Adjective and Adverb

> **Chapter focus:** position, comparison, adverb meaning, and precise Task 1 description.

## How to study this lesson

Read the explanation first, then say the examples aloud. Change one detail in each example, such as the subject, time, or place. Complete the practice without looking back and use the answer key to understand the reason for each answer. Finish by writing two sentences about your own life or city.

An **adjective** describes a noun or follows a linking verb: **a significant change** and **The change was significant**. An **adverb** modifies a verb, adjective, another adverb, or a whole clause: **changed significantly**, **highly significant**, **very carefully**, and **Fortunately, the plan worked**.

### Position and meaning

Adjectives normally come before a noun or after *be, seem, become, look,* and similar linking verbs. Several adjectives usually follow an order such as opinion, size, age, colour, origin, and material: **a useful small digital device**. Avoid adding adjectives merely to sound advanced.

Many adverbs end in *-ly*, but not all. *Fast, hard, late,* and *well* have special patterns: **a fast train**, **the train moved fast**; **She worked hard**, but **She hardly worked** means almost not at all. **Good** is an adjective; **well** is usually the adverb: **a good answer**, **answered well**.

### Comparatives and superlatives

Use *-er/-est* with many short adjectives: *faster, fastest*. Use *more/most* with longer adjectives: *more effective, most effective*. Do not write **more easier**. For comparisons, use **than**: **Online learning is more flexible than classroom learning**. Use **as + adjective + as** for equality: **The second option is as practical as the first**.

### Adverbs in IELTS writing and speaking

Place frequency adverbs before a main verb but after *be*: **People often travel** and **People are often influenced**. Sentence adverbs can come at the beginning: **Generally, the trend was upward**. Do not use an adverb to replace precise evidence. In Task 1, **increased sharply** is useful only when the data support a large change.

### Practice

Choose the correct form: (a) The policy was (high/highly) effective. (b) The number rose (dramatic/dramatically). (c) This is the (more practical/most practical) solution of the three. (d) The second method is (as efficient than/as efficient as) the first.

**Answers:** (a) **highly**, (b) **dramatically**, (c) **most practical**, (d) **as efficient as**.

## Deep lesson: describe the right thing in the right place

An adjective describes a noun or follows a linking verb: **a significant change**; **The change was significant**. An adverb modifies a verb, adjective, another adverb, or a whole clause: **changed significantly**, **highly significant**, **very carefully**, **Fortunately, it worked**.

### Form and position

Most adjectives come before a noun. Frequency adverbs usually come before a main verb but after *be*: **People often travel** and **People are often influenced**. Put an adverb close to what it modifies. **Only the manager approved the plan** does not mean the same as **The manager approved only the plan**.

### Twelve examples

1. a **rapid** increase / increased **rapidly**
2. a **careful** analysis / analysed **carefully**
3. a **good** answer / answered **well**
4. The road is **wide**.
5. The policy is **highly effective**.
6. The number rose **slightly**.
7. The second option is **more practical than** the first.
8. This is the **most reliable** method.
9. The two systems are **as efficient as** each other.
10. She worked **hard**, but she **hardly** rested.
11. **Generally**, the trend was upward.
12. The result was **surprisingly** positive.

### Comparative control

Use *-er/-est* with many short adjectives: **smaller, smallest**. Use *more/most* with longer adjectives: **more effective, most effective**. Do not combine both: not *more easier*. Use **than** after a comparative and **as…as** for equality.

### Bengali-speaker errors

**Wrong:** The number increased dramatic. **Right:** increased **dramatically**.
**Wrong:** This method is more easier. **Right:** This method is **easier**.
**Wrong:** He explained good. **Right:** He explained **well**.
**Wrong:** The policy was success. **Right:** The policy was **successful** or **a success**.

### Practice

## Professor's explanation: how to think about this grammar

Do not begin by memorising a definition. Begin by asking three questions: **What job does this form do? What meaning does it add? Where does it go in the sentence?** Bengali and English often arrange information differently, so direct translation can create mistakes. Build the English pattern first, then attach your idea to it.

## Bengali learner warning

Many Bengali speakers understand the meaning of an English sentence but omit a small grammar word because Bengali does not always need the same word. English normally needs a clear subject and a finite verb. For example, say **The number of students is increasing**, not **Number of students increasing**. Treat articles, auxiliary verbs, plural endings, and prepositions as part of the pattern, not as optional decoration.

## Three-step study method

**Step 1 — Notice:** underline the target form in every example. **Step 2 — Control:** replace only one part of the example, such as the subject or time. **Step 3 — Communicate:** use the form in a short answer about education, health, transport, technology, or the environment. If you cannot explain why you chose the form, return to Step 1.

## Practice ladder

**Level 1: Recognition.** Find the target form and name its job. **Level 2: Controlled production.** Complete or correct a sentence using the pattern. **Level 3: IELTS production.** Write a short paragraph or speak for one minute. At Level 3, clarity is more important than using many different forms.

## Revision checklist

Before moving on, ask: Can I explain the form in one simple sentence? Can I make a positive, negative, and question form where relevant? Can I use it with a past and a present example? Can I recognise the most common Bengali-speaker error? Can I use it naturally in one IELTS answer?
