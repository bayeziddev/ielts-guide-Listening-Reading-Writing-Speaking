# Grammar Foundations for IELTS

This course teaches the grammar you need to understand and produce clear English for IELTS. It begins with the smallest building blocks and gradually moves to sentence control, complex sentences, and natural linking. You do not need to memorise every rule at once. Read one chapter, study the examples aloud, complete the practice, and then use the target structure in a short IELTS answer.

## Why grammar matters in IELTS

Grammar is not a separate paper in IELTS. However, **Grammatical Range and Accuracy** is one of the four equally weighted criteria in both Writing and Speaking. Range means that you can use more than one kind of structure. Accuracy means that your structures are generally correct and that errors do not make your meaning difficult to understand. [1]

A beginner should follow this order:

1. Learn what each word does in a sentence.
2. Build accurate simple sentences.
3. Control tense, agreement, articles, and word order.
4. Join ideas with clauses and connectors.
5. Add complex structures only when they remain accurate.

> **Important IELTS principle:** A shorter correct sentence is better than a long sentence whose meaning is unclear. Complexity should improve communication, not hide mistakes.

## How to study this course

For every example, identify the subject, verb, time, and relationship between ideas. Then change one detail. For example, change *The city is growing* to *The city has grown rapidly since 2010*. This small transformation develops control.

Keep an error log with four columns: your sentence, the corrected sentence, the rule, and your new example. In Writing, review the log after every practice essay. In Speaking, record one answer and listen for missing verbs, incorrect tense, subject–verb agreement, and incomplete sentences.

## 1. Introduction to Parts of Speech

A **part of speech** is a group of words classified by the job it performs in a sentence. Traditional grammar commonly identifies nouns, pronouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, prepositions, conjunctions, and interjections. A word can belong to different classes in different sentences.

### The main word classes

| Word class | Main job | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Noun | names a person, place, thing, or idea | *student, Dhaka, pollution, freedom* |
| Pronoun | replaces a noun | *she, they, which, someone* |
| Verb | expresses an action, event, or state | *study, increase, seem* |
| Adjective | describes a noun or pronoun | *large, useful, environmental* |
| Adverb | modifies a verb, adjective, adverb, or whole clause | *carefully, very, fortunately* |
| Preposition | shows a relationship, often of time, place, or direction | *in, at, from, because of* |
| Conjunction | connects words, phrases, or clauses | *and, but, although* |
| Interjection | expresses a brief feeling or reaction | *Oh! Wow!* |

Consider the sentence: **The highly motivated students completed the difficult task carefully because the deadline was close.** *Students* and *task* are nouns; *completed* and *was* are verbs; *motivated* and *difficult* are adjectives; *highly* and *carefully* are adverbs; *because* is a conjunction; and *the* is a determiner. Determiners such as *a, an, the, this,* and *many* are also important even though they are often taught separately.

### Word families for IELTS

Academic English frequently changes one root word into several forms: *educate* (verb), *education* (noun), *educational* (adjective), and *educationally* (adverb). Build vocabulary in families, but check the spelling and the grammar of each form. In a Task 1 sentence, write **The percentage increased**, not **The percentage increase** when a verb is required.

### Practice

Identify the bold word class: (a) The **rapid** growth created pressure. (b) People **adapt** to change. (c) The policy was implemented **gradually**. (d) **Although** the plan was expensive, it was effective.

**Answers:** (a) adjective, (b) verb, (c) adverb, (d) conjunction.

## 2. Noun

A **noun** names a person, place, object, substance, activity, or idea. IELTS requires careful control of noun number, countability, articles, and word formation.

### Common noun types

A **common noun** refers to a general class (*city, student*); a **proper noun** names a particular person or place (*London, Asia*). A **concrete noun** can be experienced physically (*road, water*), while an **abstract noun** refers to an idea or quality (*development, equality*). A **collective noun** refers to a group (*team, government, population*).

### Countable and uncountable nouns

Countable nouns have singular and plural forms: *one idea, two ideas*. A singular countable noun normally needs a determiner: **A student needs feedback**, not **Student needs feedback**. Uncountable nouns are treated as substances, categories, or concepts: *information, advice, research, equipment, traffic, accommodation,* and *progress*. They do not normally take *-s*.

Use **much/little** with uncountable nouns and **many/few** with countable nouns. Use **a piece of advice**, **a piece of information**, or **an item of equipment** when you need a countable unit.

### Articles and possession

Use **a/an** for one non-specific countable thing: **A solution is needed**. Use **the** when the reader knows which thing you mean or when the noun is unique in the context: **The solution proposed by the committee was expensive**. Use no article for plural or uncountable nouns when speaking generally: **Public parks improve quality of life** and **Education is important**.

For possession, use an apostrophe: **the government’s policy**. For a plural ending in *-s*, place the apostrophe after the *s*: **students’ results**. Do not confuse **its** (possessive) with **it’s** (it is).

### IELTS word-formation warning

A noun may require a different suffix: *develop → development*, *accurate → accuracy*, *responsible → responsibility*. Spelling changes are common. In a summary-completion task, check whether the gap needs a noun and whether the answer must be singular or plural.

### Practice

Correct these sentences: (a) The government should provide more informations. (b) Student need access to library. (c) The peoples’ opinions were divided.

**Answers:** (a) The government should provide more **information**. (b) **Students need access to a library** or **A student needs access to the library**, depending on the meaning. (c) **The people’s opinions were divided**; *people* is already plural.

## 3. Pronoun

A **pronoun** stands for a noun or noun phrase. It prevents unnecessary repetition, but the reader must always be able to identify its reference.

### Main pronoun groups

**Personal pronouns** include *I, you, he, she, it, we, they* and their object forms *me, him, her, us, them*. Use the subject form before a verb: **She and I discussed the issue**. Use the object form after a verb or preposition: **The teacher advised us** and **between you and me**.

**Possessive forms** are *my, your, his, her, its, our, their* before a noun and *mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs* when the noun is omitted: **This is their proposal; that one is ours**. **Its** has no apostrophe.

**Reflexive pronouns** (*myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, themselves*) refer back to the subject: **The participants introduced themselves**. Do not use *myself* simply because it sounds formal: **Please contact me**, not **Please contact myself**.

**Relative pronouns** introduce information about a noun: *who* for people, *which* for things, and *that* for people or things in defining clauses. **The researcher who conducted the survey published the results.** Use a comma before a non-defining clause: **The survey, which was conducted online, attracted 500 responses.**

**Indefinite pronouns** include *someone, anyone, nobody, each, every, both, either,* and *neither*. *Each, every, someone,* and *nobody* are grammatically singular: **Every participant has a choice**.

### Clear reference

Avoid a pronoun when its reference is unclear: **When the device was placed beside the phone, it overheated** does not clearly say what overheated. Repeat the noun or rewrite the sentence: **The device overheated when it was placed beside the phone**.

### Practice

Choose the best form: (a) The manager asked my colleague and (I/me) to present. (b) Every applicant must bring (their/his or her) passport. (c) The city introduced a scheme, (who/which) reduced congestion.

**Answers:** (a) **me**, (b) **their** is widely used as a singular gender-neutral pronoun in modern English; **his or her** is also grammatically possible, (c) **which**.

## 4. Verb

A **verb** tells us what happens or what state exists. Every complete English clause needs a finite verb. Verbs carry information about tense, agreement, voice, and possibility.

### Verb types

An **action verb** describes an activity: *measure, reduce, construct*. A **stative verb** describes a state: *know, believe, seem, belong*. Stative verbs are not normally used in the continuous form: **I understand the problem**, not usually **I am understanding the problem**.

A **transitive verb** takes an object: **The policy reduced emissions**. An **intransitive verb** does not: **The population grew**. Some verbs can be both: **The company increased prices** and **Prices increased**.

**Auxiliary verbs** help form questions, negatives, tenses, and passive structures. The primary auxiliaries are *be, have,* and *do*. Modal verbs include *can, could, may, might, must, should, will,* and *would*. A modal is followed by the base form: **Governments should invest**, not **should invests** or **should to invest**.

### Agreement and verb patterns

The verb must agree with its subject: **The result shows** but **The results show**. In a phrase such as **The number of students is increasing**, the subject is *number*, not *students*. After *make, let,* and most modals, use the base form: **The policy made people change** and **can improve**. After a preposition, use a noun or *-ing* form: **by reducing waste**, not **by reduce waste**.

### Active and passive voice

In the active voice, the subject performs the action: **Researchers collected the data**. In the passive voice, the receiver is the focus: **The data were collected by researchers**. Use the passive when the process or result matters more than the actor, especially in Academic Task 1 process descriptions.

### Practice

Correct the verbs: (a) The results indicates a clear trend. (b) Governments should to improve public transport. (c) The data was collected from three schools. (d) This method allows students improving accuracy.

**Answers:** (a) **indicate**, (b) **should improve**, (c) **The data were collected** is the formal academic choice; *data was* is common in less formal usage when *data* means a mass of information, (d) **allows students to improve**.

## 5. Adjective and Adverb

An **adjective** describes a noun or follows a linking verb: **a significant change** and **The change was significant**. An **adverb** modifies a verb, adjective, another adverb, or a whole clause: **changed significantly**, **highly significant**, **very carefully**, and **Fortunately, the plan worked**.

### Position and meaning

Adjectives normally come before a noun or after *be, seem, become, look,* and similar linking verbs. Several adjectives usually follow an order such as opinion, size, age, colour, origin, and material: **a useful small digital device**. Avoid adding adjectives merely to sound advanced.

Many adverbs end in *-ly*, but not all. *Fast, hard, late,* and *well* have special patterns: **a fast train**, **the train moved fast**; **She worked hard**, but **She hardly worked** means almost not at all. **Good** is an adjective; **well** is usually the adverb: **a good answer**, **answered well**.

### Comparatives and superlatives

Use *-er/-est* with many short adjectives: *faster, fastest*. Use *more/most* with longer adjectives: *more effective, most effective*. Do not write **more easier**. For comparisons, use **than**: **Online learning is more flexible than classroom learning**. Use **as + adjective + as** for equality: **The second option is as practical as the first**.

### Adverbs in IELTS writing and speaking

Place frequency adverbs before a main verb but after *be*: **People often travel** and **People are often influenced**. Sentence adverbs can come at the beginning: **Generally, the trend was upward**. Do not use an adverb to replace precise evidence. In Task 1, **increased sharply** is useful only when the data support a large change.

### Practice

Choose the correct form: (a) The policy was (high/highly) effective. (b) The number rose (dramatic/dramatically). (c) This is the (more practical/most practical) solution of the three. (d) The second method is (as efficient than/as efficient as) the first.

**Answers:** (a) **highly**, (b) **dramatically**, (c) **most practical**, (d) **as efficient as**.

## 6. Preposition, Conjunction and Interjection

A **preposition** introduces a noun phrase and shows a relationship. Common relationships include place (*in a city, on the table, at school*), time (*in 2026, on Monday, at 9 a.m.*), direction (*to the station, from home*), and cause or method (*because of rain, by train*).

### Preposition patterns

Use **interested in**, **responsible for**, **depend on**, **similar to**, **different from**, and **increase in**. Learn the whole phrase rather than translating each preposition from your first language. A preposition is followed by a noun, pronoun, or *-ing* form: **interested in learning**.

Use **during + noun**: **during the lesson**. Use **while + clause**: **while the lesson was taking place**. Use **despite/in spite of + noun or -ing**: **despite the cost** and **despite being expensive**. Use **although/even though + clause**: **Although it was expensive, it was adopted**.

A **coordinating conjunction** joins equal elements: *and, but, or, so, yet, for, nor*. A **subordinating conjunction** introduces a dependent clause: *because, although, if, when, whereas, while, unless*. A **correlative conjunction** works as a pair: *both…and, either…or, neither…nor, not only…but also*.

Use a comma after an introductory dependent clause: **Although the sample was small, the result was useful.** When the independent clause comes first, a comma is often unnecessary: **The result was useful although the sample was small.** Do not join two complete sentences with only a comma. Use a full stop, semicolon, or a conjunction.

**Interjections** such as *Oh!* and *Wow!* are common in informal speech but rarely belong in IELTS Writing. In Speaking, natural reactions are acceptable, but do not rely on fillers such as *you know* to replace ideas.

### Practice

Correct the choices: (a) Despite of the rain, the event continued. (b) Although the method was cheap. It was unreliable. (c) The scheme reduced waste, it also created jobs.

**Answers:** (a) **Despite the rain** or **In spite of the rain**; (b) **Although the method was cheap, it was unreliable**; (c) **The scheme reduced waste and also created jobs** or **The scheme reduced waste; it also created jobs**.

## 7. Number and Person

**Number** tells us whether a noun or pronoun is singular or plural. **Person** tells us who is speaking: first person (*I/we*), second person (*you*), or third person (*he/she/it/they*). Number and person affect pronouns and verb agreement.

### Singular and plural control

Most nouns form the plural with *-s* or *-es*: *book/books, box/boxes*. Important irregular forms include *child/children, person/people, man/men, woman/women,* and *criterion/criteria*. Some nouns are uncountable in academic English: *advice, evidence, furniture, knowledge, luggage, machinery, research,* and *traffic*.

Compound subjects joined by **and** normally take a plural verb: **The teacher and the student are discussing the plan**. With **either…or** and **neither…nor**, the verb usually agrees with the nearer subject in careful English: **Neither the students nor the teacher is available**; **Neither the teacher nor the students are available**.

Expressions of quantity require attention: **A number of people are concerned**, but **The number of people is rising**. **Each of the options has** a different cost. Collective nouns such as *government, team,* and *family* may take a singular verb when the group is considered one unit: **The government has announced a policy**.

### Person and academic tone

First person is not automatically wrong in IELTS. **I believe** can be natural in an opinion essay, but repeated personal claims can make the writing less analytical. Use a general subject when appropriate: **This essay argues that…** In Speaking, first person is essential because you are answering about your own experience.

Avoid shifting person without reason: **If a person wants to improve, you should practise** mixes third and second person. Write **If people want to improve, they should practise** or **If you want to improve, you should practise**.

### Practice

Choose the correct form: (a) A number of factors (is/are) responsible. (b) The number of applicants (has/have) increased. (c) Each student (need/needs) feedback. (d) If someone studies consistently, (they/he) usually improves.

**Answers:** (a) **are**, (b) **has**, (c) **needs**, (d) **they** is a natural gender-neutral singular pronoun; **he or she** is also possible.

## 8. Sentence Types

A **sentence** expresses a complete thought. A clause contains a subject and a verb. An **independent clause** can stand alone; a **dependent clause** cannot.

### Four useful sentence patterns

A **simple sentence** has one independent clause: **Public transport reduces congestion.** It can still contain a compound subject or object. A **compound sentence** joins two independent clauses with a coordinating conjunction or semicolon: **Public transport reduces congestion, and it lowers emissions.**

A **complex sentence** contains one independent clause and at least one dependent clause: **Although public transport can be crowded, it reduces congestion.** A **compound-complex sentence** contains at least two independent clauses and a dependent clause: **Although public transport can be crowded, it reduces congestion, and it lowers emissions.**

Use variety for clarity, not to satisfy a formula. A strong Task 2 paragraph may begin with a simple topic sentence, explain it with a complex sentence, and finish with a specific example. Long strings of clauses increase the risk of fragments, comma splices, and unclear references.

### Sentence problems

A **fragment** is an incomplete sentence: **Because the cost was high.** Repair it by adding an independent clause: **Because the cost was high, few people adopted the scheme.** A **run-on** incorrectly joins complete sentences: **The plan was expensive it was effective.** Use a full stop or conjunction. A **comma splice** joins complete sentences with only a comma: **The plan was expensive, it was effective.**

### Word order

The basic English order is subject–verb–object: **Researchers examined the results.** Adjectives usually precede nouns, and adverbs should be placed near the word they modify. In questions, an auxiliary normally comes before the subject: **Do people support the policy?**

### Practice

Identify and repair the problem: (a) Because the sample was small. (b) The city expanded rapidly, it lacked adequate roads. (c) The survey was clear, and participants completed it quickly.

**Answers:** (a) Fragment: **Because the sample was small, the findings must be interpreted carefully.** (b) Comma splice: **The city expanded rapidly, but it lacked adequate roads.** (c) Correct compound sentence.

## 9. Tense

Tense places an event in time. Aspect shows whether an event is complete, ongoing, repeated, or connected to another time. IELTS does not reward using every tense; it rewards choosing the tense that matches the meaning.

### The core tenses

**Present simple** describes facts, routines, and regular situations: **The graph shows the change** and **Many people use public transport**. **Present continuous** describes an action in progress or a temporary trend: **The city is expanding**.

**Past simple** describes a completed past event: **The rate fell in 2015**. **Past continuous** describes an action in progress at a past time: **Researchers were collecting data when the equipment failed**.

**Present perfect** connects a past event to the present or describes an unfinished period: **The rate has increased since 2015**. Do not use it with a finished past time such as *in 2015*: write **increased in 2015**, not **has increased in 2015**. **Past perfect** places one past event before another: **The rate had fallen before the new policy was introduced**.

**Future forms** include *will* for predictions or decisions, *be going to* for intentions or evidence-based predictions, and the present continuous for arrangements: **The trend will probably continue**; **The government is going to introduce a scheme**; **The committee is meeting tomorrow**.

### Tense in IELTS tasks

Task 1 usually requires a mix of present simple for the chart introduction, past simple for finished years, and future or prediction language when a forecast is shown. Task 2 often uses present simple for general arguments, present perfect for change over time, and conditional forms for recommendations. Speaking Part 2 requires past tenses for a past story and may move to present or future when explaining why the experience matters.

### Practice

Choose the correct tense: (a) The percentage (rose/has risen) in 2010. (b) Since 2010, the percentage (rose/has risen). (c) The diagram (shows/showed) how the machine works. (d) By the time the survey began, the team (trained/had trained) the interviewers.

**Answers:** (a) **rose**, (b) **has risen**, (c) **shows** when the diagram is being described now, (d) **had trained**.

## 10. Conditional Sentence

A conditional sentence links a condition with a result. The **if-clause** gives the condition and the main clause gives the result. The clauses can change order: **If governments invest, services improve** and **Services improve if governments invest**. A comma is normally used when the if-clause comes first.

### Main conditional patterns

**Zero conditional** describes general truths: **If water reaches 100°C, it boils.** Use present simple in both clauses.

**First conditional** describes a real or possible future result: **If the government invests in rail, congestion will decrease.** Use present simple after *if*, not *will*: **If the policy succeeds**, not usually **If the policy will succeed**.

**Second conditional** describes an unlikely or imaginary present/future situation: **If I were the mayor, I would improve bus services.** In formal English, **were** can be used with all subjects in the phrase **If I were…**.

**Third conditional** describes an unreal past and its imagined result: **If the city had planned better, it would have avoided the problem.** The condition uses *had + past participle*; the result uses *would have + past participle*.

**Mixed conditionals** connect different times: **If the city had invested earlier, it would have better transport now.** Use them only when the time relationship is clear.

Other expressions include **unless** (*if not*), **provided that**, **as long as**, and **even if**. **Unless** already contains a negative meaning: **Unless the plan changes, costs will rise**; do not write **unless it does not change** when you mean the same thing.

### IELTS use

Conditionals are useful for recommendations and consequences in Task 2: **If schools teach media literacy, young people may evaluate online claims more carefully.** Do not force them into every paragraph. A correct first conditional is more valuable than a complex mixed conditional used incorrectly. [2]

### Practice

Complete the sentences: (a) If people used less plastic, the environment ___ (benefit). (b) If I ___ (be) responsible, I would fund public libraries. (c) If the company had checked the data, it ___ (avoid) the error.

**Answers:** (a) **would benefit** for an imaginary situation, or **will benefit** if the writer means a realistic future condition; (b) **were**; (c) **would have avoided**.

## 11. Right Forms of Verb

“Right form of verb” means selecting the form that fits the subject, tense, auxiliary, voice, and pattern of the sentence. In IELTS, this skill appears in every productive answer and is especially visible in Task 1 descriptions.

### A practical decision process

First, find the subject: **The percentage of households** has a singular head, *percentage*. Next, decide the time: present, past, or a period continuing to the present. Then decide whether the action is active or passive. Finally, check the verb pattern after the word before it.

Use **to + base verb** after verbs such as *want, need, decide, plan, allow someone,* and *enable someone*: **The policy enables residents to recycle**. Use **-ing** after prepositions and verbs such as *avoid, consider, recommend,* and *suggest*: **The report recommends improving access**. After *suggest*, do not write **suggest to improve** in standard academic usage; write **suggest improving** or **suggest that the city improve**.

Use the past participle after *have*: **has increased, had fallen**. Use the past participle after *be* in a passive: **was measured, is expected**. Use the present participle after *be* in a continuous form: **is increasing, was changing**.

### Frequent errors

Do not add *-s* after a modal: **can reduce**, not **can reduces**. Do not use *to* after *make* in the active pattern: **The policy made people change**. In the passive, use *to*: **People were made to change**. After **look forward to**, *to* is a preposition: **look forward to meeting you**.

### Practice

Correct the forms: (a) The graph show a gradual decline. (b) The report recommends to build more schools. (c) The equipment was install in 2020. (d) The policy has led to reduce waste.

**Answers:** (a) **shows**, (b) **recommends building** or **recommends that the government build**, (c) **was installed**, (d) **has led to a reduction in waste** or **has led to reducing waste**.

## 12. Connector and Linking Words

Connectors show how one idea relates to another. A connector cannot repair weak logic by itself. Before choosing a word, decide whether you are adding, contrasting, giving a reason, showing a result, giving an example, or ordering information.

### Meaning and grammar

Use **in addition, furthermore, moreover, and also** to add ideas. Use **however, nevertheless, whereas, while, although, and in contrast** to show contrast. Use **because, since, and as** before a clause; use **because of, due to, and owing to** before a noun phrase. Use **therefore, consequently, as a result, and thus** to show a result. Use **for example, for instance, and such as** to introduce examples, but note that **such as** is followed by a noun or noun phrase: **activities such as walking and cycling**.

Use **first, subsequently, finally, and overall** to organise stages or summaries. In Task 1, **overall** is especially useful for the overview, but the overview must state the main trend rather than merely announce that one exists.

### Punctuation and position

A transition such as **however** can begin a new sentence: **However, the policy was expensive.** It can also appear after a semicolon: **The policy was expensive; however, it was effective.** Do not join two independent clauses with a comma before *however*. With **although**, do not add **but** in the same clause: **Although the plan was costly, it worked**, not **Although the plan was costly, but it worked**.

Do not repeat **moreover, furthermore, in addition, on the other hand** in every sentence. Clear paragraph structure is more important than a high number of connectors. Use pronouns and repeated key terms to maintain cohesion naturally.

### Practice

Improve the links: (a) The scheme was costly. However it reduced waste. (b) Because of the city introduced a fee, traffic decreased. (c) Although the plan was expensive, but it was successful.

**Answers:** (a) **The scheme was costly. However, it reduced waste.** (b) **Because the city introduced a fee, traffic decreased** or **Because of the fee introduced by the city, traffic decreased.** (c) **Although the plan was expensive, it was successful** or **The plan was expensive, but it was successful.**

## 13. Phrases and Clauses

A **phrase** is a group of words without a complete subject–verb relationship. A **clause** contains a subject and a verb. Understanding the difference helps you build accurate complex sentences.

### Common phrases

A **noun phrase** has a noun as its head: **the rapid growth of urban populations**. An **adjective phrase** describes a noun: **very difficult to measure**. An **adverb phrase** modifies a verb or clause: **with considerable accuracy**. A **prepositional phrase** begins with a preposition: **in many developing countries**.

### Main clause types

An **independent clause** expresses a complete idea: **The population is growing**. A **dependent or subordinate clause** begins with a subordinating word and cannot stand alone: **because the population is growing**. An **adjective or relative clause** describes a noun: **the policy that reduced emissions**. An **adverb clause** explains time, reason, contrast, or condition: **because the policy reduced emissions**.

A **noun clause** functions like a noun: **What the report recommends is unclear** and **Researchers agree that exercise improves health**. In an IELTS essay, noun clauses can make claims precise, but they should not make sentences unnecessarily heavy.

### Reduced clauses and participles

A relative clause can sometimes be reduced: **The people living in cities** means **The people who live in cities**. Use a reduced form only when the relationship is clear. An introductory participle phrase must logically refer to the subject: **After reviewing the data, the researchers wrote a report.** The researchers reviewed the data, not the report.

### Relative clauses and punctuation

A defining relative clause identifies which person or thing: **Students who practise regularly improve**. A non-defining clause adds extra information and uses commas: **My tutor, who has taught for ten years, gave useful feedback.** Do not use *that* in a non-defining clause in standard formal writing.

### Practice

Join each pair: (a) The survey was conducted online. It attracted 800 people. (b) The policy was introduced in 2015. It reduced emissions. (c) The researchers analysed the results. They found a strong relationship.

**Possible answers:** (a) **The survey, which was conducted online, attracted 800 people.** (b) **The policy that was introduced in 2015 reduced emissions.** (c) **After analysing the results, the researchers found a strong relationship.**

## 14. Idioms

An **idiom** is a fixed expression whose meaning is not always predictable from its individual words, such as **a piece of cake** meaning “easy.” Idioms can make informal speech lively, but they are not a shortcut to a high IELTS score.

### Idioms in IELTS

In Speaking, a familiar idiom can sound natural when it fits the topic and your normal way of speaking: **Getting used to university life was a bit of a learning curve.** Do not memorise long lists and insert them into every answer. A forced idiom may sound inaccurate or rehearsed.

In Writing, avoid conversational idioms in formal essays: **The government must bite the bullet** is less appropriate than **The government must take difficult but necessary action**. Some fixed academic expressions are useful, but they are better learned as collocations: **play a role, address an issue, pose a challenge, reach a conclusion, take measures,** and **have a significant impact on**.

### Safe practice method

Learn an idiom with its meaning, register, grammar, and one natural example. Mark it as informal, neutral, or formal. Check whether it is common in British English and whether the context supports it. Never change part of an idiom unless you are certain that the variation exists.

### Useful natural expressions

For Speaking, appropriate expressions include **from my point of view**, **a turning point**, **once in a while**, **to some extent**, and **a learning curve**. For formal Writing, prefer **in the long term**, **a key factor**, **a considerable proportion**, **in terms of**, and **as a result**. These are expressions, not decorations: each must communicate a precise relationship or meaning.

### Practice

Replace the informal expression with a formal alternative: (a) The project cost an arm and a leg. (b) The policy was a game changer. (c) The plan went down the drain.

**Possible answers:** (a) **The project was extremely expensive.** (b) **The policy had a transformative effect.** (c) **The plan failed** or **The investment produced no useful result.**

## IELTS application: from grammar to a better answer

### Task 1 mini-model

**Basic version:** The number of cyclists increased. The increase was large. It happened after 2015.

**Controlled version:** **The number of cyclists increased considerably after 2015.**

**More developed version:** **After remaining relatively stable until 2015, the number of cyclists increased considerably, reaching its highest point at the end of the period.**

The developed sentence uses a participle phrase, a past simple verb, an adverb, and a result phrase. It is useful only if the data show these facts. Never add a trend that the visual does not support.

### Task 2 mini-model

**Weak:** People use cars. Cars cause pollution. The government should act.

**Controlled:** **Although private cars provide convenience, their widespread use contributes to air pollution. Therefore, governments should improve public transport so that residents have a practical alternative.**

This version uses a concessive clause, a precise verb, a connector, and a purpose clause. It remains readable because each clause has one clear job.

### Speaking mini-model

**Question:** Do you enjoy learning English?

**Answer:** **Yes, generally. I especially enjoy learning vocabulary because it helps me understand films and podcasts more easily. At first, I found listening difficult, but regular practice has made a noticeable difference.**

The answer combines a direct response, a reason, a past contrast, and a present result. It does not require rare words or complicated grammar.

## Final self-check before submitting IELTS work

Read your work once for meaning and once for grammar. Check that every sentence has a subject and a finite verb. Check subject–verb agreement, singular and plural nouns, articles, verb tense, and the form after a preposition. Circle every connector and ask whether it expresses the intended relationship. Finally, check whether each complex sentence is still easy to understand.

A realistic target is **accuracy first, controlled range second, and sophistication third**. You can reach a strong score with familiar grammar used precisely. Consistent practice, correction, and revision will improve your performance more reliably than memorising isolated rules.
